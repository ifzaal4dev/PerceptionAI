Did They Really Mean That? How AI Interpretations Shape Perceptions of Ambiguous Messages

Ifzaal Ahamed Imdad, Vikas Chowdary Meka, Vishal Meka
Advisor: Dr. Sehrish Basir Nizamani, Department of Computer Science, Virginia Tech

People now ask AI chatbots to explain text messages they are unsure about, such as a friend replying "Sure, that's fine." We tested whether the way someone words that question changes the answer they get. We wrote 20 short fictional text conversations, each ending with a message that could be read as sincere or as passive-aggressive. Using Virginia Tech's Advanced Research Computing service, we asked two models, gpt-oss-120b and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, about each conversation in three ways: a neutral question ("What did they mean?"), a negatively leading question ("Are they being passive-aggressive?"), and a positively leading question ("Are they being sincere?"). Each version was asked 10 times, for 1,200 answers in total. A third model, GLM-5.3, coded every answer for its main reading, whether it mentioned another reading, and whether it hedged. Three human coders, working in pairs, coded a sample of the answers to check it.

Leading questions pushed both models toward the negative reading. With the neutral question, 27% of gpt-oss-120b answers and 37% of DeepSeek answers read the sender as negative. With the passive-aggressive question, this rose to 48% and 53%. Asking whether the sender was sincere did not move the models the other way: negative readings rose to 60% and 51%. Leading questions also made the answers much less certain. gpt-oss-120b hedged in 3% of its answers to the neutral question and in 79% to 90% of its answers to the leading ones. The model coder agreed with the human coders about as well as the humans agreed with each other (Cohen's kappa of 0.72 vs. 0.69 for the main reading). Next, a human survey (VT IRB #26-1078) will test whether a confident or hedged AI interpretation changes how people judge the sender.
